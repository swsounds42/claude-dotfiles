# Interview Prep

Use AI the way an athlete uses a training partner: hard practice before game day, then they play the game themselves.

## The boundary — read this first

Some companies require candidates to attest that **no AI tools were used during any part of the interview process** — assessments, take-homes, live rounds — on pain of disqualification. This workflow respects that line:

- ✅ **Before the room.** Drill, practice, pressure-test, give blunt feedback. All fair game.
- ❌ **In the room, or on the take-home.** Don't produce answers, code, or deliverables the candidate submits as their own AI-free work in a process they've attested is AI-free.

That's not squeamishness. The real risk isn't a detector — it's an interviewer noticing that the live candidate doesn't match the ghostwritten one. **This boundary holds even if the candidate asks otherwise.** Offer the practice version instead: work the *approach* and the mental math so they can run it unaided.

## Inputs

- **Role and company.** Pull the saved `applications/<company>-<role>.md` if it exists.
- **The round type** — recruiter screen, hiring manager, technical/case, peer panel, executive, or a values/"bar raiser" round. Each is a different animal.
- **What's known about the format** — take-home? live case? presentation? panel? How long?

## Step 1 — Research the round, not just the company

Two things move the needle more than company facts:

**Who is in the room.** Look up the interviewer. Their background predicts what they'll probe. An engineering-heavy recruiter screens differently than a domain specialist. A cross-functional interviewer cares about how the candidate works with *their* function. Someone based in a different region will care about how the candidate handles distance and alignment.

**What the company publishes about its own process.** Many post their interview stages, what each round evaluates, and whether they send prep material. If they say they provide preparation for a technical stage — **have the candidate ask for it.** That single question is often worth more than an hour of guessing.

## Step 2 — Build the story bank

Turn real experience into tight, reusable stories. Context → Action → Result, with the candidate's *own* action isolated from the team's. For each:
- a one-line hook,
- the specific thing **they** did,
- the number it moved.

Keep them true and first-person. These are memory aids for lived experience, not scripts.

Have cold, adapted to the role:
- Two or three headline builds with numbers
- A leadership story — coaching, hiring, a person who grew
- A cross-functional conflict, and how it resolved
- **A real failure**, and what changed in how they work
- The "why this role / why now" answer

**Choose which builds lead, and know why.** The strongest number isn't always the right lead. A pipeline figure in a data role makes the candidate sound like they're applying to a different job. Match the proof to the problem the posting describes, and keep the bigger-but-less-relevant numbers in reserve for "tell me about your impact."

## Step 3 — Find the traps

Every candidate has two or three specific things that can kill a round. Name them explicitly and decide the answer *before* the call:

- **A logistics mismatch** — location, in-office expectation, start date, travel. Any hesitation reads as "will negotiate, then decline." Decide, then say it flat.
- **A genuine gap** against a stated requirement. Name it, then name the mitigation. An admission with a plan is strong; an admission alone is a hole they'll keep poking; a denial is worst of all.
- **Compensation.** Know the posted range, know local pay-transparency law (in some jurisdictions they cannot ask salary history), and anchor on the range rather than volunteering a current number.
- **Anything the résumé claims that won't survive a follow-up.** A tool listed but barely used is the classic. Calibrate the claim to the truth *before* someone asks them to walk through it.

## Step 4 — Drill

Generate the question set for this specific round, then **make the candidate answer.** Don't hand them answers.

Cover: the two-minute background angled at this role; why this role, why now, why leaving; behavioral (conflict, influence without authority, a hard call, coaching, a miss); role-specific depth; and three or four questions *they* should ask that prove they read past the posting.

## Step 5 — Pressure-test, which is the actual work

- **Mock it live.** One question at a time. Let them answer in their own words. Then give blunt feedback: where they rambled, buried the number, missed the "so what," or described a project without showing the system behind it.
- **Case rounds:** walk the *approach* and the arithmetic so they can do it unaided later. Practice cases — never the real take-home.
- **Tighten, don't script.** The output is the candidate saying it better in their own voice, not reciting a paragraph.

Have them say it out loud. Speaking it is the rep; typing it isn't.

## Step 6 — Facts cold

Flashcard what they'll need without notes: their own metrics, and the company's basics — stage, scale, recent news, competitive position, the stack. If they can't say it in the shower, they can't say it in the room.

## Step 7 — Debrief

Right after a round, capture while it's fresh:
- **What actually got asked**, in order
- **What did *not* come up** — those questions are deferred, not resolved, and they usually land harder later
- Anything learned about the org, the team, the loop, or the people
- Where they felt shaky
- For values-based processes: **exactly which values they named**, since a later round will check consistency and expect a story behind each

Write it into `applications/<company>-<role>.md`. Then re-aim the prep: a second conversation with the same interviewer is a different round than the first, and later rounds shift from narrative ("who are you") to plan ("what would you do"). When the candidate has already met the hiring manager twice, they will not be asked to walk through their background again — prepare the plan instead.

## Success criteria

- [ ] Each core story lands in under 90 seconds, number included, from memory
- [ ] Real answers exist for why-this-role and for a genuine failure
- [ ] The traps have decided answers, not improvised ones
- [ ] They've been mocked live and got specific feedback — not handed a script
- [ ] They can run the case approach unaided
- [ ] Nothing here is a deliverable to be submitted as AI-free work

## The point

Prep hard beforehand so the room is easy. A prepared candidate doesn't need anything piped in live — and the work is making that obvious to the people across the table.
